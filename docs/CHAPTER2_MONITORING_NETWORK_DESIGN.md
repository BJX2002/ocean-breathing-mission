# Chapter 2 — Monitoring Network

Status: proposed playable direction based on the NerOcean product presentation

## Communication goal

Chapter 1 teaches that a changed ecosystem is evidence, not proof of one cause.
Chapter 2 should let the player test the low-oxygen hypothesis and understand why
one short manual reading cannot represent every time, location, or depth.

The product advantage is therefore experienced through the monitoring strategy,
not delivered as an advertisement panel.

## Core mission

**Design a monitoring network that captures a pre-dawn, near-bottom low-oxygen
event at an aquaculture bay without exceeding the project budget.**

The player receives a map with three horizontal zones (bay entrance, raft edge,
inner bay), three depth bands (surface, mid-water, near-bottom), a limited budget,
and seven simulated days. The event is not revealed in advance.

## Play loop

1. Inspect the site and the Chapter 1 evidence.
2. Place sensors by location and depth.
3. Choose continuous monitoring or occasional manual sampling.
4. Advance the clock and watch the data arrive.
5. Respond to fouling, calibration drift, maintenance trips, and data gaps.
6. Identify when and where the low-oxygen event occurred.
7. Submit a cautious conclusion supported by the network data.

## Product comparison as gameplay

| System | Player-facing trade-off |
| --- | --- |
| Manual sampling | Low initial cost, but sparse measurements can miss short pre-dawn events. |
| Conventional electrode / optical sensor | Continuous data, but maintenance, calibration, fouling, and replacement visits consume budget and create gaps. |
| NerOcean network | Lower per-point and maintenance cost allows more locations and depths to remain online for longer, creating a denser 3D record. |

The simulation should not guarantee that the product solves low oxygen. It helps
the player observe the event with better spatial and temporal coverage.

## Three rounds

### Round 1 — One point is not enough

The player can afford only a few conventional instruments. A daytime surface
reading looks normal, while a short near-bottom event occurs before dawn and is
missed. Debrief: dissolved oxygen can vary strongly by hour, season, location,
and depth.

### Round 2 — Maintenance creates blind spots

Fouling and calibration tasks appear during the simulated deployment. Each boat
trip costs time and money. If the player delays maintenance, confidence in the
data falls; if the player services every instrument, coverage becomes sparse.

### Round 3 — Build the 3D network

The player deploys more low-maintenance NerOcean nodes at the bay entrance,
raft edge, and inner bay, with at least one near-bottom node. Remote data reveals
the event's timing and depth. The optimal answer is a network, not one magic
sensor.

## Scoring

- Event detection: was the low-oxygen episode captured?
- Coverage: how many important location × depth × time cells were observed?
- Data confidence: were drift, fouling, and gaps controlled?
- Total cost: instruments, maintenance trips, and downtime.
- Scientific reasoning: does the final claim match the evidence without claiming
  that one event proves a universal cause?

## Product claims requiring review

The presentation describes lower sensor price, lower maintenance cost, long
maintenance intervals, no calibration, remote monitoring, high selectivity, and
multi-point 3D monitoring. Exact percentages and service intervals should only
appear in the released game after product, engineering, and marketing review.

## Prototype boundary

The first Chapter 2 prototype needs only a placement board, a seven-day time
simulation, three sensor types, one hidden pre-dawn bottom event, and a debrief
chart. It does not need a second free-swimming dive scene.
